01The approved one

What Melanotan 1 actually is

Melanotan 1 is afamelanotide — same molecule, two names. It’s a synthetic, long-acting copy of the body’s pigment hormone (α-MSH), engineered to be selective for the pigment receptor. Approved as Scenesse, it’s a prescription implant for a rare light-sensitivity disease — not a tanning product.

Each bead is one amino acid. The highlighted tweaks turn natural α-MSH into a long-acting drug.

Three names, one molecule

Melanotan 1 (research name) = afamelanotide (generic) = Scenesse (brand). One 13-amino-acid peptide with an identity crisis — and very different reputations per name.

Selective by design

Unlike MT2’s shotgun, afamelanotide is biased toward MC1R, the pigment receptor. Fewer receptors hit means fewer whole-body surprises — the selectivity that made approval possible.

A disease drug, not a tan drug

Approved for erythropoietic protoporphyria (EPP) — a rare genetic disease where light causes excruciating pain. It’s given as a prescription implant placed by a clinician, not sold for cosmetic tanning.

Plain English

If MT2 is a skeleton key, afamelanotide is a key cut for one lock: the pigment switch. Same key blank, wildly different key — and only one of them earned a medical license, for a disease where sunlight feels like being burned from the inside.

02Light hurts

The disease behind the drug

Scenesse exists because of EPP — one of the most painful rare diseases you’ve never heard of. Understanding the disease explains why regulators said yes here and no to cosmetic tanning.

The selective sibling took the medical path: decades of trials for one rare disease.
EPP: when light burns

Erythropoietic protoporphyria is a genetic disorder where a porphyrin builds up in the blood. Minutes of sunlight trigger agonizing, burning pain lasting days — no blisters, no rash, just pain. Patients live like vampires by necessity.

Melanin as armor

More eumelanin means more built-in light protection. In EPP trials, afamelanotide increased melanin density and — crucially — pain-free time in sunlight, the outcome that mattered.

Why approval made sense

For a disease with no other treatment and life-altering pain, regulators accepted the trade-offs: nausea, moles darkening, implant-site effects. The benefit–harm math that fails for cosmetic tanning passes for EPP.

Plain English

Context is everything in medicine: the same tan that’s a frivolity at the beach is armor for someone whose blood turns sunlight into agony. Regulators didn’t approve a tanning drug — they approved pain-free daylight.

05MT1 vs MT2

Afamelanotide vs Melanotan 2

The comparison the internet constantly mangles. Same family, same tanning idea — different molecules, different selectivity, different legal and medical universes.

Afamelanotide · the scalpel

Linear 13-aa, MC1R-selective. Approved as Scenesse (EU 2014, US 2019) for EPP. Prescription implant, clinician-placed. Side effects: nausea, headache, mole darkening.

Melanotan 2 · the shotgun

Cyclic 7-aa, non-selective (MC1R/3R/4R/5R). Never approved anywhere. Gray-market vials and sprays. Bundle: tan plus nausea, appetite loss, erections, mole changes.

Why it matters

Forums constantly blur the two — “melanotan” almost always means MT2. Borrowing afamelanotide’s approved halo for MT2’s unregulated vial is the most common category error in tanning-peptide discourse.

One molecule earned approval through selectivity and trials; the other lives on forums.

Head to head: structure & receptors

Structure

Afamelanotide: linear chain of 13 amino acids — a tweaked copy of natural α-MSH ([Nle4, D-Phe7] swaps make it potent and long-lasting).
MT2: tiny cyclic ring of 7 — a compact, rugged shape that hits more targets.

Receptor activity

Afamelanotide: biased toward MC1R (the pigment receptor) — the scalpel.
MT2: fires MC1R, MC3R, MC4R, MC5R — the shotgun. MC4R/MC3R activity is where the extra effects come from.

Pigmentation

Both darken skin — that part overlaps. But afamelanotide's pigment effect was measured in trials with medical supervision; MT2's comes from uncontrolled use, often with UV overexposure stacked on top.

Sexual effects: where they come from

MT2's famous libido and erection effects trace to MC4R/MC3R signaling in the brain — receptors afamelanotide largely spares. That's why Scenesse's label reads nausea and mole-darkening, not the MT2 bundle. Same family, different wiring.

Clinical development

Afamelanotide: decades of development → EMA 2014, FDA 2019 for EPP.
MT2: researched, then abandoned by developers — never approved anywhere, never manufactured as a medicine.

Same name, different molecule: "Melanotan I" vs afamelanotide

Afamelanotide (Scenesse)

  • The actual molecule: defined 13-aa peptide, made under pharmaceutical control.
  • Prescription implant, placed by a clinician, dosed on a medical schedule.
  • Every batch tested; every patient monitored (skin surveillance included).

"Melanotan I" sold online

  • An unregulated label — vials and sprays from gray-market sellers.
  • Frequently not afamelanotide at all: wrong peptide, wrong dose, or nothing verifiable.
  • No testing, no monitoring, no connection to the approved medicine.
Plain English

"Melanotan 1" is a research nickname for afamelanotide. When a website sells you "Melanotan I," you're not buying Scenesse — you're buying an unverified powder borrowing a medicine's name. The name is the same; the molecule in the vial often isn't.

Where PT-141 fits

PT-141 (bremelanotide) · the cousin

Derived from the MT2 lineage, but aimed at MC4R — the brain receptor behind sexual function. Approved as Vyleesi (2019) for low sexual desire in premenopausal women. Tanning is not its job.

The family map

Afamelanotide → MC1R → pigment (EPP drug).
MT2 → MC1R/3R/4R/5R → pigment + extras (unapproved).
PT-141 → MC4R → desire (HSDD drug).
Three molecules, one receptor family, three different destinations.

Plain English

Calling MT2 “just like Scenesse” is like calling moonshine “just like prescription medicine” because both contain alcohol. Same chemical family, opposite universes of testing, oversight, and legal status — learn the two names.

07Who it may suit

Who it may suit — and who it may not

One established indication, one drug, and a lot of internet noise. The established lane is narrow; everything else is informational context, not guidance.

09Risks & unknowns

Risks & unknowns

An approved drug still has a risk file — it’s just a studied one. What the label says, what’s plausible, and what remains unknown.

Nausea & headache

Labeled: nausea and headache are the most common adverse effects — typically transient, and milder than MT2’s GI reputation.

Pigmentation changes

Labeled: darkening of skin, moles, and freckles. EPP patients get regular skin monitoring — the label treats mole vigilance as part of the therapy.

Implant-site effects

Labeled: pain, swelling, or irritation where the implant is placed. A clinician procedure with clinician-managed aftercare.

The melanoma question

Theoretical: stimulating melanocytes long-term raises the textbook question. No signal has emerged in EPP surveillance, but the monitored population is small — absence of evidence, carefully watched.

Decades of use

Unknown: EPP is lifelong; decades-long safety data is still accumulating. What very-long-term melanocyte stimulation does is being learned in real time, under supervision.

Off-label & counterfeit

Unknown: “afamelanotide” from unregulated sellers is frequently not afamelanotide at all. Anything outside the prescription implant is a different risk universe.

Plain English

Known: nausea, pigmentation shifts, implant-site effects — all labeled, all monitored. Theoretical: the long-term melanocyte question, watched but unanswered. Unknown: decades-out data and whatever’s in counterfeit vials. A real risk file — which is precisely what approval buys you.

10Glossary

Understanding Melanotan 1 (Afamelanotide) Terminology

Twelve terms, each with the science and a plain-English translation. Tap any term to expand it.

The scienceAfamelanotide is the international nonproprietary (generic) name of a synthetic 13-amino-acid analog of α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH), carrying norleucine at position 4 and D-phenylalanine at position 7 ([Nle4, D-Phe7]-α-MSH). These two substitutions make it more resistant to enzymatic breakdown and more potent than the native hormone. It is highly selective for the melanocortin-1 receptor (MC1R), the pigment receptor on melanocytes. In research contexts it is also known as Melanotan 1, and it is the active ingredient of the prescription implant Scenesse.

Plain EnglishAfamelanotide is the official name of the lab-made hormone copy this page is about — a 13-piece protein chain shaped to copy the body’s natural pigment-signaling hormone, with two small tweaks that make it last longer and hit the pigment receptor more precisely. It is sold under the brand name Scenesse as a prescription implant for a rare disease. “Melanotan 1” is the nickname used in research and gray-market circles.

The scienceScenesse is the brand name of afamelanotide, formulated as a subcutaneous sustained-release implant. It was approved in the European Union in 2014 and by the FDA in the United States in 2019 for erythropoietic protoporphyria (EPP) in adults, and it is not approved for cosmetic tanning. The implant is placed under the skin by a healthcare professional and releases the drug steadily over weeks. It is the only approved product containing afamelanotide.

Plain EnglishScenesse is the prescription brand name — the implant a doctor places under the skin to treat EPP. It earned its approvals for one rare disease and is not a tanning product. It is given by a healthcare professional and releases the drug slowly over weeks.

The scienceErythropoietic protoporphyria is a rare inherited disorder in which protoporphyrin IX, a light-reactive intermediate in the heme biosynthesis pathway, accumulates in red blood cells and blood. On exposure to sunlight, the accumulated protoporphyrin generates reactive oxygen species that cause severe burning pain, typically within minutes. The pain is disproportionate to any visible skin changes and can last for hours to days. It belongs to the porphyria family of heme-metabolism disorders.

Plain EnglishEPP is a rare genetic condition where a light-sensitive chemical builds up in the blood. When sunlight hits it, the chemical sparks a painful burning reaction within minutes — pain far worse than the skin looks. That is why it is called a phototoxic disorder: here, light itself acts like the toxin.

The scienceThe melanocortin-1 receptor (MC1R) is a G-protein-coupled receptor expressed primarily on melanocytes, the pigment-producing cells of the skin and hair. Activation of MC1R by α-MSH or its analogs stimulates the cAMP signaling cascade inside the cell, which increases eumelanin synthesis. MC1R is afamelanotide’s primary pharmacological target. Its selectivity for this receptor is what distinguishes afamelanotide from the non-selective Melanotan 2.

Plain EnglishMC1R is the pigment receptor — a molecular switch sitting on the surface of skin pigment cells. When it switches on, the cell gets the message to make more dark pigment. Afamelanotide is designed to press this one switch while mostly leaving the other receptors in the family alone.

The scienceEumelanin is the dark, brown-black pigment produced by melanocytes and the dominant form of melanin in human skin, hair, and eyes. It absorbs and scatters ultraviolet and visible light, providing the skin’s natural photoprotective capacity. Raising eumelanin density in the skin is the pharmacological mechanism by which afamelanotide increases light tolerance in EPP. More eumelanin means more light can be absorbed harmlessly before symptoms begin.

Plain EnglishEumelanin is the dark pigment that colors skin, hair, and eyes. It works like built-in sunscreen, soaking up light before it can do damage. More eumelanin means the skin can tolerate more sunlight — which is exactly what the EPP treatment is trying to achieve.

The scienceProtoporphyrin IX is a light-reactive porphyrin compound and a normal intermediate in the body’s heme-making pathway. In EPP, impaired ferrochelatase activity causes protoporphyrin to accumulate in red blood cells and blood instead of being converted into heme. When sunlight — including visible wavelengths, not just ultraviolet — strikes the accumulated protoporphyrin, it triggers a phototoxic reaction. That reaction produces the burning pain that defines the disease.

Plain EnglishProtoporphyrin is a light-sensitive chemical that is normally just a stepping stone on the way to making heme. In EPP it piles up in the blood instead of being finished into heme. Sunlight then hits this pileup and sets off a painful reaction — that pileup is the villain of the EPP story.

The sciencePhotoprotection is any measure that reduces light-induced damage to biological tissue. It includes sunscreen use, protective clothing, behavioral avoidance of sunlight, and — in the case of afamelanotide therapy — increased melanin density in the skin. Greater photoprotection raises the threshold of light exposure a person can tolerate before symptoms occur. In EPP care it is measured by a concrete outcome: more time in daylight without pain.

Plain EnglishPhotoprotection simply means defending skin against light damage. Sunscreen, hats, shade, and — in this case — extra melanin are all forms of it. More protection means the skin can handle more sunlight before reacting. For people with EPP, that translates into something simple: more pain-free time outdoors.

The scienceIn this context, an implant is a small solid rod containing afamelanotide, placed under the skin by a healthcare professional. It releases the drug steadily over several weeks, providing sustained exposure without the need for frequent dosing. The slow-release format is what makes Scenesse’s treatment schedule practical. It is the only approved way to receive afamelanotide — there are no approved pills, creams, or sprays.

Plain EnglishAn implant is a tiny medicine rod slipped under the skin by a doctor. It then releases the drug into the body slowly, over weeks. That slow drip is what makes the treatment schedule workable. It is the only form afamelanotide is approved in — no pills, no creams, no sprays.

The scienceAn orphan drug is a medicine developed to treat a rare disease — in the United States, generally a condition affecting fewer than 200,000 people. Regulatory frameworks such as the US Orphan Drug Act and the EU orphan designation grant incentives, including market exclusivity, to make developing such drugs economically viable. Scenesse’s orphan status reflects EPP’s rarity. Small patient populations and high development costs are why orphan drugs typically carry very high prices.

Plain EnglishAn orphan drug is a medicine made for a rare disease — one too few people have to make a normal market out of it. Governments offer special incentives so companies bother to develop them at all. It is why a drug used by a small number of patients carries a very large price tag.

The scienceMelanotan 2 is a cyclic peptide that activates multiple melanocortin receptors non-selectively, in contrast to the MC1R-selective afamelanotide. It was never approved as a medicine and exists only as an unregulated gray-market product. Because it acts on several receptors rather than one, its effect profile is broader and less predictable. It is frequently confused with Melanotan 1 (afamelanotide), but the two are distinct molecules with different legal status and evidence bases.

Plain EnglishMelanotan 2 is the wilder sibling: a cyclic peptide that hits several pigment-family receptors instead of one. It was never approved as a medicine and is sold only on the gray market. Its effects are broader and less predictable than Melanotan 1’s. Same family name, different molecule, different legal status — don’t mix them up.

The scienceSkin surveillance is the scheduled dermatological monitoring for changes in moles, nevi, and pigmentation during afamelanotide therapy. It is a labeled requirement of Scenesse treatment: because the drug pharmacologically stimulates melanocytes, those cells are watched for pigmentary changes. Full-body skin examinations are performed periodically during the treatment course. It is part of the official treatment program, not an optional add-on.

Plain EnglishSkin surveillance means regular mole-check appointments built into the treatment. Since the drug works by stimulating pigment cells, doctors keep a close eye on those cells for changes. It is part of the official treatment program — required, not optional.

The scienceIn pharmacology, selectivity describes how preferentially a drug binds its intended receptor over other, related receptors. Afamelanotide’s high selectivity for MC1R means it activates the pigment pathway with minimal activity at the other melanocortin receptors. This selectivity is the key pharmacological distinction between afamelanotide and the non-selective Melanotan 2. It is the reason their effect profiles differ so markedly despite the similar names.

Plain EnglishSelectivity means hitting the intended target while sparing everything nearby. Afamelanotide mostly presses the one pigment switch — that is why it behaves differently from Melanotan 2’s skeleton key. In drug design, selectivity is usually what separates a clean effect from a mess of unintended ones.
